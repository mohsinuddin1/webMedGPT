Are you feeling constantly fatigued, experiencing brain fog, or noticing unusual muscle weakness? These are classic symptoms of vitamin and mineral deficiencies. 

When you visit your doctor complaining of low energy, one of the first things they will order is a vitamin panel. This blood test typically checks your levels of Vitamin D, Vitamin B12, and Iron. 

If you have just received your lab results and are wondering what "Ferritin" or "25-OH Vitamin D" means, this guide will help you understand your vitamin deficiency blood tests.

## 1. Vitamin D (25-Hydroxyvitamin D)

Vitamin D is crucial for bone health, immune system function, and mood regulation. Because our bodies produce it primarily through sun exposure, deficiency is extremely common, especially in northern regions during the winter months.

On your lab report, this test is usually listed as **25-OH Vitamin D** or **25-Hydroxycalciferol**.

### What Do the Numbers Mean?
*Note: Reference ranges can vary slightly by laboratory.*

- **Deficiency:** Less than 20 ng/mL
- **Insufficiency:** 20 - 29 ng/mL
- **Optimal / Normal:** 30 - 50 ng/mL (Some functional medicine doctors recommend 40 - 60 ng/mL)
- **Toxicity:** Greater than 100 ng/mL

### Symptoms of Low Vitamin D
If your results show a deficiency, you might be experiencing:
- Fatigue and sleep issues
- Bone or muscle pain (and a higher risk of stress fractures)
- Frequent illness or infections
- Depression or low mood

## 2. Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin)

Vitamin B12 is essential for nerve function, brain health, and the production of red blood cells. Since B12 is naturally found almost exclusively in animal products, vegans and vegetarians are at a high risk of deficiency. Older adults and those with digestive issues (like Crohn's disease) also struggle to absorb B12.

### What Do the Numbers Mean?

- **Deficiency:** Less than 200 pg/mL
- **Borderline / Low Normal:** 200 - 300 pg/mL (Many people experience symptoms in this range)
- **Optimal / Normal:** 300 - 900 pg/mL

### Symptoms of Low Vitamin B12
A severe B12 deficiency can lead to irreversible nerve damage if left untreated. Symptoms include:
- "Pins and needles" sensation or numbness in hands and feet
- Brain fog, memory loss, and difficulty concentrating
- Extreme fatigue (Megaloblastic anemia)
- A swollen, inflamed tongue

> **Pro Tip:** If your B12 is borderline, your doctor might also check your **Methylmalonic Acid (MMA)** or **Homocysteine** levels. High levels of these markers can confirm a true B12 deficiency at the cellular level.

## 3. The Iron Panel (Ferritin, Iron, and TIBC)

Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional deficiency worldwide and the leading cause of anemia. However, reading an iron panel is tricky because "Iron" is rarely the most important number on the page. 

Here is what you should look for:

### Ferritin
Ferritin is a protein that stores iron in your cells. **This is the most important marker for iron deficiency.** It tells you how much iron your body has in its "savings account."
- **Low Ferritin (usually &lt; 30 ng/mL):** Indicates your iron stores are depleted. You can have low ferritin even before you develop full-blown anemia.
- **High Ferritin:** Can indicate inflammation, infection, or a genetic condition called hemochromatosis (iron overload).

### Serum Iron
This measures the amount of iron currently floating in your blood (your "checking account"). It fluctuates wildly depending on what you ate that day, which is why doctors rely more on Ferritin.

### TIBC (Total Iron-Binding Capacity)
This measures how well proteins in your blood can bind to iron. 
- **High TIBC:** Usually means your iron stores are low (your body is creating more proteins to try and "catch" any available iron).

### Symptoms of Low Iron (Anemia)
- Exhaustion and shortness of breath (especially when exercising)
- Pale skin and brittle nails
- Cold hands and feet
- Dizziness or lightheadedness
