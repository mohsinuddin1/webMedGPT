Opening a sexual health portal and seeing words like *reactive*, *non-reactive*, *titer 1:8*, or *index 1.4* can trigger immediate anxiety—especially when you are trying to make sense of your **STI test results** privately before a clinic calls back. Because sexually transmitted infections are often asymptomatic, laboratory reports rely on specialized microbiology and immunology shorthand rather than simple "yes" or "no" labels.

Understanding the difference between a screening signal, a confirmed diagnosis, and a test taken too early inside a **window period** puts you back in control. If you are reviewing other flagged numbers on your portal at the same time, see our guide on [abnormal blood test results and when to worry](/blog/abnormal-blood-test-results-when-to-worry).

## What Do "Reactive," "Non-Reactive," and "Equivocal" Mean on an STI Report?

On an STI or STD lab report, **non-reactive** (or negative) means no target antigen, antibody, or pathogen genetic material was detected above the assay threshold, **reactive** (or positive) means a marker was detected and may need confirmatory testing, and **equivocal** (or indeterminate) means the signal fell in an inconclusive gray zone requiring a repeat draw.

According to [NIH MedlinePlus STI testing guidance](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/sexually-transmitted-infection-sti-tests/), laboratories choose wording based on the underlying technology—molecular DNA/RNA amplification versus blood antibody/antigen serology:

| Report Term | What the Lab Measured | Clinical Meaning | Recommended Next Step |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Non-Reactive / Not Detected** | Antibody, antigen, or pathogen DNA/RNA below cutoff | No evidence of infection *at the time of sampling* | Confirm you tested **after** the infection's window period; retest if exposure was recent |
| **Reactive / Detected** | Target antigen, antibody, or genetic sequence found | Active infection, past resolved infection (e.g., syphilis/HSV IgG), or screening false positive | Check whether a **confirmatory reflex test** ran automatically; book treatment or follow-up |
| **Equivocal / Indeterminate** | Signal sits inside borderline gray zone (e.g., HSV IgG `0.90–1.09`) | Early seroconversion, waning immunity, or nonspecific cross-reaction | Repeat the blood test in **2–4 weeks** or use a higher-specificity confirmatory assay |
| **Titer (e.g., `1:4`, `1:32`)** | Serial blood dilution strength (`RPR` / `VDRL`) | Measures antibody concentration in syphilis; tracks active disease vs. cure | Compare to baseline titer—a **4-fold drop** (e.g., `1:32` → `1:8`) confirms cure |
| **Index / `S/CO` Ratio** | Signal-to-cutoff ratio (HSV IgG, HIV combo, Hepatitis) | Numerical strength above or below the assay's positive cutoff (`≥ 1.0` or `≥ 1.10`) | Low-positive `HSV-2 IgG` values (`1.10–3.00`) need confirmation to rule out false positives |
| **Quantitative Viral Load** | Virus copies (`copies/mL` or `IU/mL`) | Active viral replication in `HIV-1 RNA` or `HBV/HCV` blood panels | Guides antiretroviral or antiviral therapy; `Undetectable` (`< 20–50 copies/mL`) means `U=U` for HIV |

## How Do Window Periods Affect Negative STI Test Results?

The **window period** is the interval between potential sexual exposure and the earliest date a specific lab assay can reliably detect the infection. Testing too early—before bacteria multiply enough or your immune system builds detectable antibodies—produces a **false negative** even when infection is present.

As documented by [CDC HIV testing guidelines](https://www.cdc.gov/hiv/testing/index.html) and [CDC STI testing recommendations](https://www.cdc.gov/sti/testing/index.html), every pathogen and testing method follows a distinct timeline:

- **Chlamydia & Gonorrhea (`NAAT`):** Urine or site-specific swabs (vaginal, endocervical, throat, rectal) usually turn positive **7 to 14 days** after exposure; testing at day 2 or 3 risks missing an early infection.
- **HIV Nucleic Acid Test (`NAT` / Viral RNA):** Directly detects viral RNA in venous blood **10 to 33 days** post-exposure.
- **HIV 4th-Generation Antigen/Antibody (`Ag/Ab` Combo):** Lab venous blood draws detect `p24` viral protein plus HIV antibodies **18 to 45 days** after exposure, whereas rapid finger-stick `Ag/Ab` tests require **18 to 90 days**.
- **HIV Antibody-Only (`3rd-Gen` / Oral Self-Tests):** Require **23 to 90 days** for antibody concentrations to cross the detection threshold.
- **Syphilis Serology (`RPR` / `TP-PA`):** Typically becomes detectable **3 to 6 weeks** after exposure, though full exclusion after a high-risk encounter extends to **90 days**.
- **Herpes (`HSV-1` / `HSV-2` IgG Blood Test):** Antibodies can take **2 to 12 weeks** (sometimes up to 16 weeks) to develop, whereas a **PCR swab** of an active blister detects viral DNA immediately.

> **72-Hour Emergency PEP Rule:** If you believe you were exposed to HIV within the past **72 hours**, do **not** wait for a window period to pass. Contact an urgent care clinic, sexual health center, or emergency department immediately to ask about **Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (`PEP`)**, which must start as soon as possible and no later than 72 hours after exposure.

## How Are Specific STI Lab Tests Read? (Chlamydia, Gonorrhea, HIV, Syphilis, Herpes & Hepatitis)

Modern sexual health panels combine molecular swabs or urine checks with blood serology and liver enzymes. Knowing which biomarker your report actually measured—and whether both US conventional and SI international units appear—prevents common misreadings.

| Infection | Common Lab Assay | Negative / Normal Reference | Positive / Reactive Threshold & Caveats |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Chlamydia** *(C. trachomatis)* | `NAAT` (first-catch urine or mucosal swab) | `Not Detected` / `Negative` | `Detected` confirms active localized infection at that anatomical site; see [chlamydia test guide](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/chlamydia-test/) |
| **Gonorrhea** *(N. gonorrhoeae)* | `NAAT` (urine, vaginal, pharyngeal, or rectal swab) | `Not Detected` / `Negative` | `Detected` confirms infection; throat/rectal infections require extra-genital swabs because urine misses them |
| **HIV-1 / HIV-2** | `4th-Gen Ag/Ab` combo + `HIV-1/2` differentiation + `HIV RNA` | `Non-Reactive` (`S/CO < 1.00`)<br />`HIV RNA`: `Not Detected` | Reactive screen triggers automatic differentiation; viral load reported in `copies/mL` (`< 20–50` undetectable; `1 IU/mL ≈ 0.5–1.7 copies/mL` by assay) |
| **Syphilis** *(T. pallidum)* | `RPR` / `VDRL` (non-treponemal) + `TP-PA` / `CIA` (treponemal) | `Non-Reactive` | Reactive `RPR` (`1:1`, `1:8`, `1:64`) needs treponemal confirmation; treponemal tests stay positive for life even after cure |
| **Herpes (`HSV-1` / `HSV-2`)** | Lesion `PCR/NAAT` swab or type-specific `IgG` blood test | `IgG Index < 0.90` *(Negative)*<br />`0.90–1.09` *(Equivocal)* | `IgG ≥ 1.10` is positive, **but** [CDC herpes guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/herpes/testing/index.html) warns `HSV-2 IgG` scores `1.10–3.00` have high false-positive rates and need confirmatory testing |
| **Hepatitis B (`HBV`)** | `HBsAg`, `anti-HBs`, `anti-HBc`, `ALT`/`AST` | `HBsAg`: `Non-Reactive`<br />`anti-HBs`: `≥ 10 mIU/mL` (`≥ 10 IU/L`) = immune | Positive `HBsAg` indicates active infection; `ALT` (`7–56 U/L` / `0.12–0.93 µkat/L`) tracks liver inflammation — see [liver function test guide](/blog/understanding-liver-function-test-results) |
| **Hepatitis C (`HCV`)** | `HCV Antibody` with reflex `HCV RNA` | `Non-Reactive`<br />`HCV RNA`: `Not Detected` (`< 15 IU/mL`) | Reactive `HCV Ab` + `Not Detected` RNA means past cleared infection; detectable `HCV RNA` (`IU/mL`) confirms active infection |

### Why Urine Tests Alone Can Miss Chlamydia or Gonorrhea

As explained in [NIH MedlinePlus urinalysis and STI resources](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/gonorrhea-test/), a **first-catch urine sample** (the initial 20–30 mL of urine after holding your bladder for 1–2 hours, rather than a midstream clean-catch sample used for UTIs) checks only the urinary tract. If you engage in oral or receptive anal sex, `Chlamydia` or `Gonorrhea` can reside exclusively in the throat or rectum while a urine `NAAT` returns `Not Detected`. Standard routine urinalysis dipsticks (check our [urinalysis results guide](/blog/understanding-urinalysis-urine-test-results)) check white blood cells and nitrites for urinary tract infections—they do **not** screen for STIs.

### Why Syphilis Reports Include Two Different Tests

According to [NIH MedlinePlus syphilis testing guidelines](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/syphilis-tests/), diagnosing syphilis requires pairing two distinct antibody tests:

1. **Treponemal Tests (`TP-PA`, `FTA-ABS`, `EIA`/`CIA`):** Detect antibodies specific to *Treponema pallidum*. Once positive, they typically remain positive for life—even decades after successful antibiotic treatment—so they cannot distinguish a new infection from a cured one.
2. **Non-Treponemal Tests (`RPR` or `VDRL`):** Measure non-specific antibodies linked to active tissue inflammation and report a **titer** (such as `1:16` or `1:32`). Because conditions like pregnancy, lupus, or recent viral infections can cause a **biological false positive** `RPR`, labs pair `RPR` with a treponemal test. After penicillin treatment, doctors track `RPR` titers downward—a fourfold decline (for example, `1:32` dropping to `1:8`) confirms treatment worked.

### Why Herpes Blood Screening Has a False-Positive Trap

The CDC and [WHO STI guidelines](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/sexually-transmitted-infections-(stis)) generally advise **against** routine `HSV-1/HSV-2` blood screening in people without symptoms. Avoid `IgM` herpes blood tests altogether—they cross-react frequently and cannot determine when infection occurred. Even type-specific `HSV-2 IgG` assays produce frequent false positives when the numerical index falls between **`1.10` and `3.00`**; without visible sores to swab by `PCR`, low-positive `IgG` results should be verified with a second confirmatory test (such as Biokit or Western blot).

## What Should You Do After Receiving Your STI Results?

Whether your lab report brings relief or an unexpected flag, taking structured clinical steps protects both your own health and your partners:

1. **Verify Your Collection Date Against the Window Period:** Compare your test date to your most recent potential exposure. If you tested too early, schedule a follow-up draw once the window closes.
2. **Check Every Site Tested and Every Pathogen Listed:** Confirm whether your "full panel" actually included `HIV`, `syphilis`, `hepatitis B/C`, and extra-genital swabs—many basic clinic panels only run chlamydia and gonorrhea urine `NAAT`.
3. **Start Prescribed Antibiotics Completely for Curable Bacterial STIs:** Chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, and trichomoniasis are curable with targeted prescription therapy. Avoid sexual contact until you and your partner(s) complete treatment and wait the recommended **7 days** post-single-dose or post-therapy.
4. **Confirm Reflex and Follow-Up Testing Schedules:** After chlamydia or gonorrhea treatment, the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sti/testing/index.html) recommends retesting in **3 months** to catch reinfection. After syphilis treatment, follow scheduled `RPR` titer checks at 6 and 12 months.
5. **Discuss Prevention Options (`PrEP`, `DoxyPEP`, Vaccination):** Ask about `Hepatitis B` and `HPV` vaccination, daily or on-demand `HIV PrEP`, or `DoxyPEP` if appropriate for your risk profile.

## When Do STI Symptoms Require Urgent Emergency Care?

Most STIs cause mild symptoms or none at all, yet untreated bacterial infections or acute viral seroconversion can occasionally escalate into emergencies requiring immediate hospital evaluation.

> **Emergency Red Flags:** Seek urgent medical care or go to the nearest emergency department (call **911** in the US/Canada, **999** in the UK, **112** in the EU, or **000** in Australia) if you develop severe lower abdominal or pelvic pain with high fever and vomiting (possible pelvic inflammatory disease or ectopic pregnancy), sudden painful testicular swelling, severe headache with neck stiffness, vision changes, or confusion, widespread painful blistering rash with inability to urinate, or sudden flu-like fever with rash and swollen glands within days to weeks of a high-risk HIV exposure.

## Questions to Ask Your Doctor or Sexual Health Clinic

Bringing clear questions to a GP, urologist, gynecologist, or sexual health nurse (such as a UK NHS GUM clinic or local public health center) ensures nothing gets missed:

- **"Were all tests run outside their window periods for my most recent exposure, or do I need a repeat draw?"**
- **"Did my reactive screening result include a confirmatory test (such as `HIV-1/2` differentiation, `HCV RNA`, `TP-PA`, or confirmatory `HSV-2`), and what is my exact titer or viral load?"**
- **"Should I have throat or rectal `NAAT` swabs in addition to a urine or vaginal swab?"**
- **"How long should I abstain from sex after finishing treatment, and do I need a 3-month retest?"**
- **"How can my partner(s) access expedited partner therapy or confidential testing, and am I protected against Hepatitis B and HPV?"**

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between reactive and non-reactive on an STI test?

Non-reactive (negative) means the laboratory test did not detect antibodies, antigens, or pathogen DNA/RNA above its cutoff threshold. Reactive (positive) means the screening test detected a target marker, though screening tests such as `RPR` for syphilis or `4th-generation HIV` screens always require confirmatory follow-up testing to confirm an active diagnosis.

### Can an STI test be negative if I still have an infection?

Yes. Testing too soon after exposure—during the **window period**—can return a false negative because bacterial counts, viral `p24` antigen, or antibodies have not yet reached detectable levels. Testing the wrong anatomical site (for example, urine only when infection is in the throat or rectum) can also miss localized chlamydia or gonorrhea.

### How soon after exposure is a 4th-generation HIV blood test accurate?

According to the CDC, a laboratory-drawn venous **4th-generation HIV antigen/antibody test** reliably detects most infections between **18 and 45 days** after exposure because it looks for both early `p24` viral protein and HIV antibodies. Rapid finger-prick antigen/antibody tests can take up to **90 days** to be fully conclusive.

### Does a positive RPR blood test always mean I have active syphilis?

No. Because `RPR` detects non-specific antibodies rather than the syphilis bacterium itself, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or other infections can occasionally cause a biological false positive. Doctors always pair `RPR` with a specific treponemal test (`TP-PA` or `CIA`) and monitor `RPR` titers (`1:32` → `1:8`) to confirm active infection versus cured past infection.

### Why did my herpes blood test show a low positive `HSV-2 IgG` result?

Type-specific `HSV-2 IgG` blood tests with index values between **`1.10` and `3.00`** carry a high false-positive rate—especially in people with no history of genital sores. Clinical guidelines recommend verifying low-positive results with a secondary confirmatory assay or swabbing any active lesion directly with a `PCR/NAAT` test.
