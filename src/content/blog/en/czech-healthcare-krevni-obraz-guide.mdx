Navigating medical testing in the Czech Republic can feel disorienting if you are unfamiliar with the local system. Whether you are an expat residing in Prague or Brno, a cross-border worker, or someone assisting family members with Czech healthcare paperwork, reading a Czech medical lab printout (*výsledkový list laboratorního vyšetření*) requires understanding unique clinical conventions.

Czech laboratories utilize distinct terminology—such as *krevní obraz (KO)*, *sedimentace FW*, and *jaterní testy*—and report routine organ enzymes in **microkatals per liter (µkat/l)**, a metric unit rarely used in North America or Western Europe. Furthermore, accessing tests involves statutory insurance funds like **VZP** and doctor requisition slips called *žádanky*.

What do the abbreviations on your Czech lab printout mean? How do enzymes measured in `µkat/l` translate to standard international units (`U/l`)? And when does a flagged value warrant a call to your general practitioner (*praktický lékař*)?

This plain-English medical guide breaks down Czech laboratory diagnostics into clear, actionable facts to help you interpret your health records with confidence.

## 1. How Blood Testing Works in the Czech Healthcare System

Czech outpatient care operates primarily through statutory public health insurance (*veřejné zdravotní pojištění*), complemented by accessible private walk-in diagnostic networks.

### 1. The Public System: Praktický Lékař & Žádanka o Vyšetření
- **Primary Care Gatekeeper (*Praktický lékař pro dospělé*):** In the Czech Republic, your general practitioner acts as the coordinator of outpatient care. To have routine diagnostic tests covered by public health insurance, a doctor must issue an official requisition slip (**žádanka o laboratorní vyšetření**).
- **Insurance Coverage (Zdravotní pojišťovny):** Diagnostic tests ordered for medical indications are 100% covered without co-pays by public insurance funds. The largest insurer is **VZP (Všeobecná zdravotní pojišťovna — code 111)**, alongside departmental funds such as **VoZP (201)**, **ČPZP (205)**, **OZP (207)**, and **ZP MV ČR (211)**. Guidelines for routine screening panels follow the [VZP preventive healthcare schedule](https://www.vzp.cz/pojistenci/prevence).
- **Periodic Preventive Checkups (*Preventivní prohlídka*):** Every adult registered with a Czech GP is entitled to a free comprehensive preventive examination once every two years. This includes routine urinalysis, fasting blood glucose (from age 18), and lipid profile testing at designated ages (18, 30, 40, 50, and 60).
- **Requisition Coding:** Your *žádanka* includes your personal identification number (*rodné číslo* or assigned insurance ID) and diagnostic ICD-10 codes (*kód diagnózy MKN-10*) justifying each requested biomarker.

### 2. Private Walk-In Testing as a Self-Payer (*Samoplátce*)
- If you wish to check specific biomarkers without waiting for a GP appointment, you can visit collection centers (*odběrová pracoviště*) operated by major accredited laboratory networks such as **Synlab**, **EUC Laboratoře**, **Aeskulab**, or **Spadia**.
- No physician referral is required for self-payers (*samoplátci*). You simply select the desired panel (e.g., *balíček Prevence*, *Štítná žláza*, or *Zdravý vegetarián*), pay directly at reception, and receive your results securely via email or SMS within 24–48 hours.

## 2. Complete Blood Count: Decoding the Czech Krevní Obraz (KO)

The most commonly ordered blood test in Czech healthcare is the **Krevní obraz (KO)**, the clinical equivalent of a Complete Blood Count (CBC). To understand the biological role of each blood cell lineage, review our foundational [guide to reading blood test results](/blog/how-to-read-blood-test-results).

Czech laboratories adhere to national analytical protocols published by the [Czech Society of Clinical Biochemistry (ČSKB)](https://www.cskb.cz/doporuceni/) and international hematology standards cataloged by [NIH MedlinePlus Complete Blood Count monographs](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/complete-blood-count-cbc/).

| Parameter (Czech Label) | Standard Abbreviation | What It Evaluates | Standard Czech Reference Range (Adults) | Common Units |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Leukocyty** | **WBC** / Leu | White blood cells; defense against infection | **4.0 – 10.0** | 10⁹/l |
| **Erytrocyty** | **RBC** / Ery | Red blood cells; oxygen transport | **4.0 – 5.8** (Men)<br />**3.8 – 5.2** (Women) | 10¹²/l |
| **Hemoglobin** | **Hb** / HGB | Oxygen-binding protein in erythrocytes | **135 – 175** (Men)<br />**120 – 160** (Women) | **g/l** *(not g/dL)* |
| **Hematokrit** | **Hct** / HCT | Proportion of whole blood made up of RBCs | **0.40 – 0.50** (Men)<br />**0.35 – 0.47** (Women) | Decimal fraction *(or %)* |
| **Střední objem erytrocytu** | **MCV** | Average volume and size of red blood cells | **82 – 98** | fl (femtoliters) |
| **Barvivo erytrocytu** | **MCH** | Average amount of hemoglobin per RBC | **28 – 34** | pg (picograms) |
| **Barevná koncentrace** | **MCHC** | Mean concentration of hemoglobin in packed RBCs | **320 – 360** | **g/l** |
| **Šíře distribuce erytrocytů** | **RDW** | Variation in red blood cell size (anisocytosis) | **10.0 – 15.2** | % |
| **Trombocyty** | **PLT** / Tro | Platelets; primary cellular blood clotting | **150 – 400** | 10⁹/l |

> [!NOTE]
> **Czech Unit Note — Hemoglobin in `g/l`:** While American and Western European reports frequently express hemoglobin in `g/dL` (e.g., 14.5 g/dL), Czech laboratories always report hemoglobin in **g/l** (e.g., 145 g/l). To convert, simply divide the Czech number by 10 (145 g/l = 14.5 g/dL). Similarly, hematocrit in Czechia is typically reported as a decimal ratio (e.g., `0.42`) rather than a percentage (`42%`).

### Understanding the Leukocyte Differential (*Diferenciální rozpočet*)
When a doctor orders *Krevní obraz s diferenciálem*, the laboratory analyzes the five distinct types of white blood cells. For a detailed breakdown of how immune fractions differentiate viral from bacterial illness, explore our explainer on [CBC differential white blood cell types](/blog/understanding-cbc-differential-wbc-types).

1. **Neutrofily (Segmenty & Tyče):** Primary frontline defense against acute bacterial infections. In Czech reports, neutrophils are divided into:
   - **Segmenty (*Neutrophil segments*):** Mature cells (normal: 50–70% or 2.0–7.0 × 10⁹/l).
   - **Tyče (*Neutrophil bands*):** Immature cells (normal: 0–4% or < 0.5 × 10⁹/l). A marked rise in *tyče* is referred to as *posun doleva* ("shift to the left"), signaling an acute bacterial defense surge.
2. **Lymfocyty (Lymfo):** Viral defense and adaptive antibodies (normal: 20–45% or 1.2–3.4 × 10⁹/l).
3. **Monocyty (Mono):** Scavenger macrophages cleaning cellular debris (normal: 2–10% or 0.1–0.9 × 10⁹/l).
4. **Eozinofily (Eo):** Specialized allergic and parasitic defense (normal: 1–5% or 0.03–0.5 × 10⁹/l).
5. **Bazofily (Bazo):** Histamine-releasing inflammatory cells (normal: 0–1% or < 0.1 × 10⁹/l).

### Sedimentace Erytrocytů: The Czech FW Test
On many Czech lab requisitions, you will notice the test **FW (Sedimentace erytrocytů)**, named after the *Fahraeus-Westergren* technique. 
- It measures how many millimeters red blood cells settle at the bottom of a vertical pipette in one hour (reported as **mm/hod**).
- **Normal reference values:** Men < 10–15 mm/1. hod; Women < 20 mm/1. hod.
- An elevated FW indicates non-specific systemic inflammation, infection, or tissue injury. Because FW reacts slowly, modern Czech clinicians frequently pair or replace it with **CRP (C-reaktivní protein)**, which rises and falls much faster in acute illness.

## 3. Basic Biochemistry (*Základní Biochemie*): The Czech Enzyme Quirk

Beyond hematology, Czech physicians routinely order a panel called **Základní biochemie** (basic biochemistry). This panel evaluates renal filtration, hepatic metabolism, electrolytes, and blood sugar.

### The Enzyme Unit Difference: Microkatals (`µkat/l`) vs. International Units (`U/l`)
The most common point of confusion on Czech lab slips is the measurement unit for enzymes. While most international laboratories report liver, pancreatic, and heart enzymes in **International Units per liter (U/l)** or **IU/L**, the Czech Republic and Slovakia officially use the SI unit **microkatal per liter (µkat/l)** (1 katal = catalytic conversion of 1 mole of substrate per second).

```
   ==================================================================
   CZECH ENZYME CONVERSION FORMULA:
   
          1 µkat/l  ≈  60 U/l        |        1 U/l  ≈  0.0167 µkat/l
   ==================================================================
   Example: An ALT of 0.60 µkat/l on a Czech printout corresponds to:
            0.60 × 60 = 36 U/l (within normal physiological range).
   ==================================================================
```

Here is how common biochemical markers appear on Czech laboratory reports:

| Biomarker (Czech Label) | Abbreviation | Clinical Purpose | Normal Range in Czech Units (Adults) | International Unit Equivalent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Glukóza (Glykémie)** | **GLU** | Blood sugar screening & diabetes monitoring | **3.9 – 5.6 mmol/l** | 70 – 100 mg/dL |
| **Kreatinin** | **KREA** | Muscle breakdown marker; primary kidney filter index | **62 – 106 µmol/l** (Men)<br />**44 – 80 µmol/l** (Women) | 0.7 – 1.2 mg/dL *(conversion: 88.4 µmol/l = 1 mg/dL)* |
| **Močovina (Urea)** | **UREA** | Protein nitrogen excretion product | **2.5 – 8.3 mmol/l** | 7 – 20 mg/dL BUN |
| **Kyselina močová** | **KM** / Uric | Evaluated for gout (*dna*) & kidney stones | **200 – 420 µmol/l** | 3.4 – 7.0 mg/dL |
| **ALT (Alaninaminotransferáza)** | **ALT** | Primary liver cell integrity enzyme | **< 0.80 µkat/l** (Men)<br />**< 0.60 µkat/l** (Women) | < 48 U/l (Men)<br />< 36 U/l (Women) |
| **AST (Aspartátaminotransferáza)** | **AST** | Liver, cardiac, and muscle cellular enzyme | **< 0.70 µkat/l** (Men)<br />**< 0.55 µkat/l** (Women) | < 42 U/l (Men)<br />< 33 U/l (Women) |
| **GGT (Gama-glutamyltransferáza)** | **GGT** | Biliary tract excretion & alcohol sensitivity | **< 1.10 µkat/l** (Men)<br />**< 0.70 µkat/l** (Women) | < 66 U/l (Men)<br />< 42 U/l (Women) |
| **ALP (Alkalická fosfatáza)** | **ALP** | Biliary obstruction & bone turnover | **0.70 – 2.20 µkat/l** | 42 – 132 U/l |
| **Bilirubin celkový** | **BIL** | Breakdown product of hemoglobin; liver clearance | **5.0 – 21.0 µmol/l** | 0.3 – 1.2 mg/dL |
| **C-reaktivní protein** | **CRP** | Acute systemic inflammatory marker | **< 5.0 mg/l** | < 0.5 mg/dL |

## 4. How to Interpret Czech Laboratory Flags and Symbols

When you receive your physical printout or download your PDF from a patient portal (e.g., *MojeEUC* or *Synlab Online*), the software automatically flags results falling outside statistical reference boundaries:

- **`+` / `-` Symbols:** A plus sign indicates a value above the upper reference limit (*nad normou*); a minus sign indicates a value below the lower limit (*pod normou*).
- **`H` and `L` Letters:** Representing *High* (vysoká) and *Low* (nízká).
- **Bold Print or Colored Highlighting:** Automatically applied by the laboratory information system (LIS) to catch the clinician's eye during rapid review.
- **Reference Intervals (*Referenční meze*):** Printed in the column adjacent to your result, adjusted for your age and sex.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The 95% Statistical Rule:** Reference ranges in Czech laboratories are calibrated to include 95% of a healthy reference population. By mathematical definition, **5% of perfectly healthy individuals will have results falling slightly outside the reference bracket**. An isolated minor deviation (e.g., an ALT of 0.85 µkat/l when the cutoff is 0.80) rarely indicates severe organ disease. To learn how physicians distinguish harmless fluctuations from true red flags, consult our clinical guide on [abnormal blood test results and when to worry](/blog/abnormal-blood-test-results-when-to-worry).

## 5. Emergency Red Flags: When to Seek Immediate Medical Help

Most out-of-range lab findings are non-urgent matters to discuss at your next GP visit. However, critical laboratory deviations (*kritické / panické hodnoty*) require immediate hospital evaluation.

> **Emergency Red Flags:** Call **155** (Zdravotnická záchranná služba) or **112**, or report immediately to the nearest hospital emergency department (*urgentní příjem / pohotovost*), if your blood test results show any of the following critical extremes accompanied by symptoms:
> - **Profound Anemia:** Hemoglobin **< 70 g/l** accompanied by dizziness, shortness of breath, or pale skin.
> - **Severe Thrombocytopenia:** Platelets **< 20 × 10⁹/l** with spontaneous nosebleeds, blood in urine, or petechiae (*drobný tečkovitý výsev na kůži*).
> - **Severe Neutropenia:** Absolute neutrophil count **< 0.5 × 10⁹/l** with an oral fever over 38.0°C (febrile neutropenia requiring immediate intravenous antibiotics).
> - **Acute Hyperkalemia:** Serum Potassium **> 6.5 mmol/l**, creating an immediate risk of fatal cardiac arrhythmia.
> - **Acute Renal Failure:** Creatinine exceeding **300–400 µmol/l** with sudden cessation of urine output (*anurie*).

## 6. Practical Czech Phrases & Questions for Your Doctor

When following up with your *praktický lékař* or specialist regarding your laboratory findings, use these targeted Czech questions:

1. *"Jsou tyto odchylky v krevním obraze pouze mírné, nebo vyžadují doplňující vyšetření?"*  
   *(Are these blood count deviations mild, or do they require follow-up testing?)*
2. *"Může mít na zvýšené jaterní testy vliv lék nebo doplněk stravy, který užívám?"*  
   *(Could a medication or dietary supplement I am taking be causing these elevated liver enzymes?)*
3. *"Doporučujete kontrolní odběr krve za několik týdnů, nebo je stav stabilní?"*  
   *(Do you recommend a follow-up blood draw in a few weeks, or is the condition stable?)*
4. *"Potřebuji na základě těchto výsledků žádanku k odbornému lékaři (např. hematolog, nefrolog)?"*  
   *(Do I need a referral to a specialist based on these results?)*

## 8. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### What is the difference between "krevní obraz" and "biochemie" in Czech medicine?
*Krevní obraz (KO)* is a hematological test that analyzes the cellular components of your blood—red blood cells, hemoglobin, hematocrit, platelets, and white blood cells. *Biochemie* (biochemical analysis) tests the liquid serum or plasma for dissolved chemicals, electrolytes, and metabolic markers including glucose, urea, creatinine, liver enzymes (ALT, AST), and cholesterol.

### Why do Czech lab results measure liver enzymes in µkat/l instead of U/l?
The Czech Republic and Slovakia are among the few countries that strictly adhere to the international System of Units (SI) for enzyme activity, using microkatals per liter (`µkat/l`). Most international labs use International Units (`U/l`). To convert Czech enzyme numbers to standard U/l, multiply the value by approximately 60 (e.g., 0.50 µkat/l × 60 ≈ 30 U/l).

### How often do Czech health insurance funds (VZP, OZP) pay for blood tests?
Under statutory Czech health insurance, standard diagnostic blood tests are fully covered whenever medically indicated by your physician. In addition, healthy adults are covered for a comprehensive preventive checkup (*preventivní prohlídka*) with their registered GP every two years, which includes fasting glucose and scheduled lipid screenings.

### What should I do if a value has a "+" or "H" on my Czech report?
A `+` or `H` indicates that the result exceeds the upper statistical cutoff of that laboratory's reference bracket. Minor elevations are frequently temporary and caused by benign factors such as vigorous exercise, mild dehydration, recent viral infections, or rich meals. Contact your prescribing GP to review the findings in the context of your overall clinical history.
