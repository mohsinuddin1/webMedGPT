A liver function test (LFT), also known as a hepatic panel, is a blood test that measures various enzymes, proteins, and substances produced by your liver. When the liver is inflamed, damaged, or infected, these substances spill into the bloodstream, altering the normal ranges on your lab report.

Your liver is your body’s primary filtration and metabolic powerhouse, performing over 500 vital functions—from filtering toxins and breaking down medications to producing bile for digestion. An LFT is the best window into how well this essential organ is performing. 

## Why Did My Doctor Order a Liver Panel?

Liver function tests are commonly ordered to:
- Check for liver damage from infections (like Hepatitis B or C).
- Monitor the side effects of medications (such as statins, acetaminophen, or certain antibiotics).
- Investigate symptoms of liver disease (jaundice, dark urine, or abdominal pain).
- Monitor existing liver conditions, such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) or cirrhosis.

## Key Liver Enzymes Explained

Enzymes are proteins that help speed up chemical reactions in the body. The liver contains thousands of them, but three are primarily checked during a routine LFT: ALT, AST, and ALP.

### ALT (Alanine Transaminase)
ALT is an enzyme found almost exclusively in the liver, making it the most specific indicator of liver injury. 

| Marker | What It Measures | Normal Range (Adults) |
|---|---|---|
| **ALT** | Enzyme involved in breaking down proteins | 7–55 U/L (Units per liter) |

**What affects ALT levels:**
When liver cells are damaged—due to fatty liver disease, alcohol consumption, viruses, or toxins—they leak ALT into the bloodstream. A high ALT level is a classic sign of acute or chronic liver inflammation.

### AST (Aspartate Transaminase)
Unlike ALT, AST is found not only in the liver but also in the heart, muscles, kidneys, and brain. 

| Marker | What It Measures | Normal Range (Adults) |
|---|---|---|
| **AST** | Enzyme involved in amino acid metabolism | 8–48 U/L |

**What affects AST levels:**
While AST rises alongside ALT during liver damage, an elevated AST with a normal ALT might suggest heart or muscle injury rather than a liver problem. 

### The AST/ALT Ratio
Doctors often compare the levels of AST and ALT to determine the underlying cause of liver damage:
- **Ratio &lt; 1 (ALT is higher than AST):** Often suggests non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) or viral hepatitis.
- **Ratio > 2 (AST is more than double ALT):** Strongly associated with alcohol-related liver disease.

### ALP (Alkaline Phosphatase)
ALP is an enzyme found in high concentrations in the liver and bones. In the liver, it is concentrated in the bile ducts.

| Marker | What It Measures | Normal Range (Adults) |
|---|---|---|
| **ALP** | Enzyme related to bile ducts and bone | 40–129 U/L |

**What affects ALP levels:**
High ALP levels often indicate a blockage in the bile ducts (cholestasis), gallstones, or bone diseases. Pregnant women and growing children naturally have higher ALP levels.

## Other Essential Liver Markers

A comprehensive hepatic panel checks more than just enzymes. It also evaluates proteins and waste products:

| Marker | What It Measures | Normal Range | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Total Bilirubin** | Waste product from red blood cell breakdown | 0.1–1.2 mg/dL | High levels cause jaundice (yellowing of skin/eyes) and indicate the liver is failing to process waste. |
| **Albumin** | Protein made by the liver | 3.5–5.0 g/dL | Low levels suggest chronic liver disease, as a damaged liver struggles to produce enough protein. |
| **Total Protein** | All proteins in the blood | 6.0–8.3 g/dL | Abnormal levels can indicate liver or kidney disorders, or nutritional deficiencies. |

> **Important:** Normal ranges can vary slightly depending on the specific laboratory analyzing your blood. Always refer to the reference ranges printed on your lab report.

## 🚨 Red Flags: When to Seek Immediate Medical Attention

> **Important:** Seek emergency medical care or call 911 if your abnormal liver tests are accompanied by any of the following severe symptoms:

- **Jaundice:** Sudden yellowing of your skin or the whites of your eyes.
- **Severe Abdominal Pain:** Especially in the upper right side of your abdomen.
- **Swelling (Ascites):** Rapid accumulation of fluid in your abdomen, causing a distended belly.
- **Dark Urine or Pale Stools:** Urine the color of dark tea or cola, combined with clay-colored stools.
- **Confusion or Disorientation:** A sign of hepatic encephalopathy (toxin buildup in the brain).

## Questions to Ask Your Doctor

If your liver function test results are flagged as abnormal, consider asking your doctor the following questions:
1. **"Are my liver enzymes mildly, moderately, or severely elevated?"**
2. **"Does my AST/ALT ratio suggest a specific cause for the liver stress?"**
3. **"Could any of my current medications, supplements, or herbal remedies be causing this?"**
4. **"Do I need an ultrasound to check for fatty liver or gallstones?"**
5. **"Should I undergo screening for Hepatitis B or C?"**
6. **"When should we repeat this blood test to see if the levels have normalized?"**

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does it mean if my ALT is high but AST is normal?
Mildly elevated ALT with normal AST is very common and is frequently associated with early-stage non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). It can also be caused by recent weight gain, poor diet, or certain over-the-counter medications. Your doctor will likely recommend lifestyle changes and a follow-up test.

### Can drinking alcohol the night before affect my liver test?
Yes. Heavy alcohol consumption right before a blood test can temporarily elevate liver enzymes, particularly AST and GGT. For the most accurate results, it is best to avoid alcohol for at least 24 to 48 hours before a liver function test.

### How can I lower my liver enzymes naturally?
If your elevated enzymes are due to fatty liver or lifestyle factors, you can often lower them by losing weight, adopting a Mediterranean-style diet, exercising regularly, eliminating alcohol, and avoiding processed foods high in refined sugars and trans fats.

### Are elevated liver enzymes always a sign of liver failure?
No. Mildly elevated liver enzymes are common and are often a sign of temporary liver stress (such as fighting a viral infection or processing a new medication) rather than permanent liver failure. However, they should never be ignored, as chronic inflammation can lead to permanent damage over time.
