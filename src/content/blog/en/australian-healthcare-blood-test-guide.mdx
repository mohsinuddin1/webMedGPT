Navigating pathology testing in Australia is remarkably streamlined once you understand the interconnected roles of Medicare, General Practice (GP) clinics, and digital health records. Whether you are an Australian citizen, a permanent resident, or an expat on a working visa, receiving a pathology request form often leads to immediate questions: Will Medicare cover the cost? Can you take your form to any collection centre? And how do you access your results online?

In Australia, diagnostic blood testing is officially referred to as **pathology**. Reports are generated by accredited laboratory networks and uploaded digitally to **My Health Record**, which you can access securely via **myGov** or the official Australian Digital Health app. Furthermore, Australian laboratories adhere to standard SI units—reporting haemoglobin in **grams per litre (g/L)** and electrolytes in **millimoles per litre (mmol/L)**—which differ from US conventions.

Understanding your Australian pathology report allows you to participate actively in your clinical care. This evidence-based guide breaks down how Medicare bulk billing works, the legal freedom of choice regarding pathology collection centres, how to read your Full Blood Count (FBC) and biochemistry results, and what out-of-range flags really mean.

## 1. How Blood Testing Works in the Australian Healthcare System

Australia's diagnostic healthcare ecosystem combines universal public funding through Medicare with an extensive network of accredited public and private pathology providers.

```
       [ Patient Consults GP / Medical Practitioner ]
                            │
            ┌───────────────┴───────────────┐
            ▼                               ▼
    [ Bulk-Billed Request ]         [ Private / Gap Billing ]
            │                               │
    Pathology provider accepts      Provider charges above the
    MBS benefit as full payment;    MBS schedule fee; patient
    Zero out-of-pocket cost         pays out-of-pocket gap
            │                               │
            └───────────────┬───────────────┘
                            ▼
    [ Freedom of Choice: Patient Visits ANY Collection Centre ]
    (Sonic, Healius, Australian Clinical Labs, Sullivan Nicolaides, etc.)
                            │
                            ▼
    [ Accredited Pathology Laboratory Analysis ]
                            │
            ┌───────────────┴───────────────┐
            ▼                               ▼
    [ Immediate Upload ]            [ 5-Day Clinical Hold ]
    Routine bloods (FBC, U&Es,      Complex histology, biopsies,
    LFTs, Lipids, Fasting Glucose)  and genetic investigations
            │                               │
            └───────────────┬───────────────┘
                            ▼
    [ Secure Access via My Health Record / myGov / MedGPT ]
```

### The Rule of Choice: Any Form, Any Laboratory
When your GP prints a pathology referral slip, it often features the logo and branding of a commercial pathology network (such as **Sonic Healthcare**, **Douglass Hanly Moir**, **Sullivan Nicolaides**, **Healius / Laverty Pathology**, or **Australian Clinical Labs**). 

Under the Commonwealth *Health Insurance Act 1973*, you have complete **freedom of choice**. You are legally entitled to take that request form to **any accredited pathology collection centre** in Australia. You do not have to use the laboratory company printed on the paper.

### Bulk Billing vs. Private Gap Fees
- **Bulk Billing:** Under [Services Australia Medicare bulk billing guidelines](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/bulk-billing), if a pathology practice bulk bills your tests, they accept the Medicare rebate directly from the Commonwealth government as full payment. You pay **$0 out of pocket**.
- **Private Billing (The Gap Fee):** Some private pathology providers or specialized diagnostic panels (such as specialized hormone assays, pre-employment screenings, or certain genetic panels) are not fully subsidized under the Medicare Benefits Schedule (MBS). Always ask the phlebotomist at the collection desk whether your requested tests will be completely bulk-billed.

### My Health Record: How and When Results Appear
Under the [Australian Digital Health Agency pathology report rules](https://www.digitalhealth.gov.au/initiatives-and-programs/my-health-record/consumers/pathology-reports), accredited laboratories upload diagnostic reports directly to your national electronic record:
1. **Immediate Access for Routine Bloods:** Standard tests—such as Full Blood Counts, kidney panels, liver enzymes, and lipid profiles—are available to view immediately once uploaded by the diagnostic laboratory.
2. **5-Day Delay for Sensitive Pathology:** Anatomical pathology, cytology, cancer biopsies, and genetic screening reports have a mandatory **5-day consumer access hold**. This delay ensures your prescribing doctor has adequate time to review the findings and discuss them with you directly before you view the raw medical report.

## 2. Full Blood Count (FBC): Decoding Australian Haematology

In Australian clinical practice, a complete blood workup is called a **Full Blood Count (FBC)**. To understand how blood cells are formed in the bone marrow, explore our foundational [complete blood count interpretation guide](/blog/how-to-read-blood-test-results).

Australian laboratories report haematological cell counts and indices aligned with international standards published by [NIH MedlinePlus Complete Blood Count monographs](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/complete-blood-count-cbc/):

| Biomarker | Australian Lab Label | Clinical Function | Standard Australian Adult Reference Interval | Units |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Haemoglobin** | **Hb** | Oxygen-carrying metalloprotein in erythrocytes | **130 – 180** (Men)<br />**115 – 165** (Women) | **g/L** *(US uses g/dL)* |
| **Red Cell Count** | **RCC** / RBC | Total concentration of circulating erythrocytes | **4.5 – 6.0** (Men)<br />**3.8 – 5.2** (Women) | 10¹²/L |
| **Haematocrit** | **Hct** / PCV | Proportion of blood volume occupied by RBCs | **0.40 – 0.52** (Men)<br />**0.35 – 0.47** (Women) | L/L *(or %)* |
| **Mean Cell Volume** | **MCV** | Average physical size and volume of RBCs | **80 – 100** | fL (femtolitres) |
| **Mean Cell Haemoglobin** | **MCH** | Average weight of haemoglobin inside each RBC | **27 – 34** | pg (picograms) |
| **White Cell Count** | **WCC** / WBC | Total circulating immune and defensive cells | **4.0 – 11.0** | 10⁹/L |
| **Platelets** | **PLT** | Cell fragments responsible for primary hemostasis | **150 – 400** | 10⁹/L |

> [!NOTE]
> **Australian Measurement Units:** Australian pathology uses **g/L** for haemoglobin (e.g., 142 g/L). If comparing with American sources using `g/dL`, divide by 10 (142 g/L = 14.2 g/dL). Similarly, red cell volume is reported in femtolitres (fL), and platelets are reported in thousands per microlitre expressed as `10⁹/L`.

### The White Cell Differential (WCC Breakdown)
When your GP orders an FBC with automated differential, the laboratory classifies the circulating white cells into five distinct subpopulations. For a detailed breakdown of immune cell functions, consult our guide on [CBC differential and white blood cell breakdown](/blog/understanding-cbc-differential-wbc-types):

1. **Neutrophils (2.0 – 7.5 × 10⁹/L):** The primary immune responders fighting acute bacterial infections.
2. **Lymphocytes (1.0 – 4.0 × 10⁹/L):** Viral defense, antibody production (B-cells), and cell-mediated immunity (T-cells).
3. **Monocytes (0.2 – 1.0 × 10⁹/L):** Phagocytic scavenger cells that clear tissue debris.
4. **Eosinophils (0.0 – 0.5 × 10⁹/L):** Cells involved in allergic responses, asthma, and parasitic defense.
5. **Basophils (0.0 – 0.2 × 10⁹/L):** Histamine-releasing inflammatory mediators.

## 3. Routine Biochemistry in Australia: U&Es and LFTs

Australian GPs commonly request **U&Es** (Urea & Electrolytes, also termed **EUC**: Electrolytes, Urea, and Creatinine) alongside **LFTs** (Liver Function Tests) to assess internal organ function.

### Urea & Electrolytes (U&Es / EUC Panel)
Australian laboratories measure creatinine in **micromoles per litre (µmol/L)** and electrolytes in **millimoles per litre (mmol/L)**. To understand kidney filtration and glomerular filtration rate, see our [kidney function test explainer](/blog/how-to-read-kidney-function-test-results).

| Biomarker | Australian Abbreviation | Clinical Purpose | Standard Adult Reference Interval | Units |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Sodium** | **Na** | Fluid balance, nerve transmission, and osmolarity | **135 – 145** | mmol/L |
| **Potassium** | **K** | Cardiac conduction and neuromuscular excitability | **3.5 – 5.2** | mmol/L |
| **Chloride** | **Cl** | Acid-base balance and extracellular fluid volume | **95 – 110** | mmol/L |
| **Bicarbonate** | **HCO3** | Systemic blood pH buffering | **22 – 32** | mmol/L |
| **Urea** | **Urea** | Dietary protein nitrogen excretion product | **3.0 – 8.0** | mmol/L *(not BUN)* |
| **Creatinine** | **Creat** | Skeletal muscle metabolic byproduct; renal index | **60 – 110** (Men)<br />**45 – 90** (Women) | **µmol/L** *(88.4 µmol/L = 1 mg/dL)* |
| **eGFR** | **eGFR** | Estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (CKD-EPI) | **> 90** *(normal filtration)* | mL/min/1.73m² |

### Liver Function Tests (LFTs)
Australian pathology reports enzyme activities in **Units per litre (U/L)** and bilirubin in **micromoles per litre (µmol/L)**:

| Liver Biomarker | Abbreviation | Diagnostic Role | Reference Interval (Adults) | Common Units |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Alanine Aminotransferase** | **ALT** | Specific marker of hepatocellular injury | **< 45** (Men)<br />**< 35** (Women) | U/L |
| **Aspartate Aminotransferase** | **AST** | Enzyme present in liver, heart, and skeletal muscle | **< 40** (Men)<br />**< 35** (Women) | U/L |
| **Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase** | **GGT** | Biliary excretion and alcohol sensitivity index | **< 60** (Men)<br />**< 35** (Women) | U/L |
| **Alkaline Phosphatase** | **ALP** | Bile duct obstruction and bone turnover marker | **30 – 110** | U/L |
| **Total Bilirubin** | **Bilirubin** | Haemoglobin breakdown pigment; liver clearance | **3 – 20** | µmol/L |
| **Albumin** | **Alb** | Major circulating plasma protein synthesized by liver | **35 – 50** | g/L |

## 4. Understanding Flags and Symbols on Australian Pathology Reports

When you download your pathology PDF from My Health Record or receive a printed report, the laboratory information software highlights results falling outside calibrated reference intervals:

- **Asterisk (`*`) or Bold Font:** Signifies that the value falls outside the laboratory's statistical reference interval.
- **Letters `H` (High) and `L` (Low):** Indicate the direction of numerical deviation.
- **Reference Intervals:** Displayed in the adjacent column, customized to your biological sex and age bracket.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The 95% Population Principle:** Reference intervals are established using the middle 95% of a healthy population. Mathematically, **1 in 20 healthy individuals (5%) will show a slightly abnormal result** without any underlying disease. An isolated, minor elevation (such as an ALT of 48 U/L or a platelet count of 415 × 10⁹/L) is frequently benign and transient. To explore when an out-of-range finding is clinically significant, read our guide on [abnormal blood test results and when to worry](/blog/abnormal-blood-test-results-when-to-worry).

## 5. Emergency Red Flags: When to Seek Immediate Australian Medical Care

Most pathology findings require non-urgent review with your GP. However, critical deviations (**panic values**) require immediate hospital evaluation.

> **Emergency Red Flags:** Call **000** (Triple Zero) for an ambulance or proceed immediately to the nearest hospital Emergency Department if your blood test results show any of the following critical extremes accompanied by symptoms:
> - **Profound Anaemia:** Haemoglobin **< 70 g/L** accompanied by dizziness, chest pain, or severe shortness of breath.
> - **Critical Thrombocytopenia:** Platelets **< 20 × 10⁹/L** with spontaneous gum bleeding, hematuria, or petechiae (a pinpoint red or purple skin rash).
> - **Febrile Neutropenia:** Absolute Neutrophil Count **< 0.5 × 10⁹/L** with a body temperature exceeding 38.0°C (requires immediate intravenous antibiotic therapy).
> - **Severe Hyperkalaemia:** Serum Potassium **> 6.5 mmol/L** (creates an acute risk of fatal cardiac dysrhythmia).
> - **Acute Kidney Injury:** Creatinine exceeding **300–400 µmol/L** with sudden reduction or cessation of urination (*anuria*).

## 6. Practical Questions to Ask Your Australian GP

Prepare for your follow-up appointment by bringing these targeted, actionable questions:

1. *"Are my flagged results mild fluctuations, or do they indicate an underlying health issue?"*
2. *"Could any of my current medications, over-the-counter supplements, or recent workouts have influenced these numbers?"*
3. *"Do you recommend re-testing these markers in 4 to 8 weeks to assess the trend, or is no further action needed?"*
4. *"Based on these findings, do I need a referral to a specialist (e.g., haematologist, endocrinologist, or nephrologist)?"*
5. *"Are there any lifestyle or dietary adjustments I should prioritize before our next review?"*

## 8. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Can I take my pathology request form to any collection centre in Australia?
Yes. Under the Commonwealth *Health Insurance Act 1973*, Australian patients have full freedom of choice. You can present any valid pathology request form to any accredited collection centre—such as Sonic Healthcare, Healius, or Australian Clinical Labs—regardless of the laboratory branding printed on the form.

### How long does it take for blood test results to appear in My Health Record?
Routine blood tests—including Full Blood Counts, electrolyte panels, kidney function tests, and liver enzymes—are typically uploaded and viewable immediately once finalized by the laboratory. Complex or sensitive diagnostic reports (such as biopsies, cytopathology, or genetic testing) have a mandatory 5-day delay before consumer release to allow your GP to discuss the results with you first.

### What is the difference between an FBC and a CBC?
There is no clinical difference. **Full Blood Count (FBC)** is the standard clinical term used in Australia, the United Kingdom, and New Zealand. **Complete Blood Count (CBC)** is the term preferred in North America. Both tests evaluate red blood cells, white blood cells, haemoglobin, haematocrit, and platelets.

### Are blood tests completely free under Medicare bulk billing?
When a pathology provider bulk bills your tests, they accept the standard Medicare benefit as full payment, resulting in zero out-of-pocket expenses. However, if a clinic or private provider charges above the Medicare schedule fee, or if you request tests not covered by the Medicare Benefits Schedule (MBS), you will be charged an out-of-pocket gap fee. Always confirm billing policies before your blood draw.
